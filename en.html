Let's start with the basics

Not every community is a cult. But some really do control people.

A destructive group doesn't have to look like something out of a movie. It can be a church, a prayer community, a personal-development group, an online "family," a gym built around a guru, or an MLM company. What they share is this: they gradually take control of how you think, what you feel, who you spend time with, and what you do.

🧠 It's not about faith

This test doesn't judge your religion, worldview, or spirituality. It evaluates methods of control — and those can appear in any group, religious or secular.

🔍 A starting point, not a verdict

The result is a signal, not a diagnosis. It's meant to help you notice patterns worth talking about with someone you trust.

💬 Built from lived experience

This project grew out of academic work on the BITE model and personal experience of leaving a group with cult-like characteristics.

The tool

What is the BITE model?

BITE stands for Behavior, Information, Thought, Emotion — four areas in which destructive groups take control over their members. The model was created by Stephen Hassan, an American therapist who, as a young man, was himself a member of the Unification Church (the so-called "Moonies"). After leaving the group, he devoted his career to studying psychological manipulation and helping people entangled in destructive communities.

B

Behavior control

Behavior Control

The group regulates your daily life: where you live, how you dress, who you spend time with, how you spend money, how much time you give to the group. Your decisions require leaders' approval, and disobedience is punished — openly, or through the withdrawal of relationships.

  • Mandatory meetings and activities, with consequences for missing them
  • Rules about clothing, appearance, diet, sleep, and free time
  • Restricting contact with people outside the group, including family
  • Major life decisions require the leader's or elders' approval

Attending meetings and taking part in preaching is a duty — neglecting it can get you labeled "bad association." Witnesses are also encouraged to limit contact with people outside the community, including family members who don't believe.

"Don't look for excuses to communicate with a disfellowshipped relative, for example through text messages or emails."
I

Information control

Information Control

The group decides what information reaches you. Critical sources are discouraged or forbidden, and people who share them are portrayed as enemies, liars, or "sick." Sometimes the group hides its own history or controversies.

  • Discouraging members from reading or watching material critical of the group
  • Labeling critics as "apostates," "enemies," or "mentally ill"
  • Hiding inconvenient facts about the group's history or finances
  • Encouraging members to report other members' doubts to leaders

Literature from outside the organization — including academic publications — is presented as spiritually dangerous. The only acceptable source of knowledge is meant to be the Watch Tower Society's own publications.

"Trying to refute the arguments of apostates or of those critical of Jehovah's organization will do us no good (…) it is wrong and constitutes a spiritual danger."
T

Thought control

Thought Control

The group teaches you to think in a certain way: black-and-white divisions into "us and them," ready-made phrases instead of your own reflection, and doubts treated as sin, weakness, or an "evil influence." Over time it becomes hard to think critically about the group itself.

  • Dividing the world into the "true/enlightened" and "everyone else, who is mistaken"
  • Doubt equated with lack of faith, betrayal, or illness
  • Repetitive slogans and phrases that are hard to explain to outsiders
  • Rituals (prayer, singing, meditation) used to "drown out" difficult thoughts

The world is divided into the organization (which holds "the one truth") and everyone else — under Satan's influence. Doubting the teachings is presented as dangerous.

"Raising doubts is one of Satan's most effective methods."
E

Emotion control

Emotion Control

The group manipulates your emotions — instilling guilt, shame, and fear (of punishment, condemnation, losing loved ones), while also offering a sense of belonging and love — but a conditional one, dependent on obedience and commitment.

  • Intense guilt for not meeting the group's expectations
  • Fear of punishment, condemnation, or disaster after leaving
  • The threat of losing relationships with loved ones who remain in the group (shunning)
  • Self-worth made dependent on the group's approval

People who leave lose contact with loved ones who remain in the organization — including immediate family. Leaving is bound up with intense guilt and fear of rejection by God and the community.

"A hardened sinner must be disfellowshipped — cut off from contact with Jehovah's clean people."

About me

PW
🎓 Psychology student, University of Humanities and Economics in Łódź 📖 Author of an academic analysis of the BITE model 🕊️ Survivor of the Jehovah's Witnesses

My name is Paweł Wasiak. I grew up as a Jehovah's Witness — in an organization I believed, for many years, to be the only right path and the only safe place in the world. Mandatory meetings, door-to-door preaching, rules covering nearly every area of life, fear of doubt and of "the world outside" — that was my everyday reality for years.

Leaving the organization wasn't a single event — it was a long process. First came individual questions I couldn't answer within the framework I'd been taught. Then a growing awareness of just how much my thoughts, emotions, and decisions had been shaped by the group for years, rather than by myself. That experience — and how long it took me to understand the mechanisms that had been acting on me — was the direct reason I decided to study psychology at the University of Humanities and Economics in Łódź.

During my studies I came across Stephen Hassan's BITE model, and I finally felt I had a tool that could name what I hadn't been able to name for years. I wrote an academic article about it, analyzing — using Jehovah's Witnesses as an example — exactly what behavior, information, thought, and emotion control look like in practice. You can read the full text below.

I built this site for people who are today where I was a few years ago: sensing that something is wrong, but not yet having the words or the tools to name it. If that's you — you are not alone.

Academic paper

How to recognize a cult or destructive group using the BITE model — the case of Jehovah's Witnesses

The full text of the article this site is based on.

Expand and read the full article ↓
Paweł Wasiak ORCID: 0009-0004-6555-3156 e-mail: pwasiak30@gmail.com

Abstract

This article presents the application of Stephen Hassan's BITE model (Behavior, Information, Thought, Emotion Control) as a tool for identifying the characteristics of cults and destructive groups. The Jehovah's Witnesses are analyzed as an example of a religious organization that, in many respects, meets the mind-control criteria defined by the model. The aim of the paper is to demonstrate the usefulness of the BITE model in assessing potentially destructive practices within religious communities.

Keywords: BITE model, Stephen Hassan, mind control, psychological manipulation, destructive groups, Jehovah's Witnesses, religious cults, cult psychology, psychological support, post-cult trauma

Introduction

In recent decades, awareness has grown of groups that, through intensive control, influence individuals and lead them into psychological, emotional, and social subjugation. Stephen Hassan, an expert in psychological manipulation, developed the BITE model as a tool for diagnosing this type of practice.

Before becoming one of the leading experts on psychological manipulation and cults, Hassan was himself a member of a destructive group — the Unification Church (also known as the "Moonies"). The experiences he went through as a young adult under indoctrination and intense group control became the foundation of his later therapeutic and academic work. After freeing himself from the cult, Hassan devoted his life to studying methods of mind control and helping people entangled in destructive relationships with cult-like groups. His BITE model is the summation of decades of clinical work, research, and his own experience as a former cult member.

Jehovah's Witnesses, although formally recognized as a legal religious community, are frequently identified by former members and researchers as an organization with the characteristics of a destructive cult.

Methodology

The aim of this article is to apply the BITE model to analyze the practices and structures functioning within the Jehovah's Witness organization. The study used a case-study method, which allowed for an in-depth analysis of a specific group within the framework of the psychological-manipulation model. The main sources of data were accounts from former members, academic literature, and the organization's own official publications (including the Watchtower magazine). For each of the four elements of the BITE model, specific practices or organizational norms were identified that may exemplify the use of mind control. The article does not aim to offer an objective assessment of the Jehovah's Witness religion; rather, it is analytical in character and draws on available data from a psychological perspective.

Analysis of the BITE model using Jehovah's Witnesses as an example

1. Behavior Control

The Jehovah's Witness organization precisely regulates the daily behavior of its members. Prohibitions include, among others, celebrating birthdays, observing national and religious holidays, military service, blood transfusions, and contact with people outside the community, even family members. Such restrictions can lead to social isolation and dependence on the group. Examples of these techniques:

Mandatory meetings and gatherings: Jehovah's Witnesses are required to regularly attend meetings held at Kingdom Halls and to take part in the preaching work, which is not merely a recommendation but an obligation. Failing to fulfill this obligation can lead to exclusion or ostracism within the community (one can be labeled "bad association," which results in other members of the group distancing themselves from that person).

"What picture do those who make a practice of missing congregation meetings present? Is spiritual stagnation not evident in them? Do they not find it difficult to produce the fruitage of the spirit? Who most often experience conflicts, setbacks, and a tendency to grumble and complain? And who most often comes to meetings unprepared or falls asleep during the talk? Such ones are usually overtaken by spiritual sluggishness."

Rules governing everyday behavior: There is a range of rules covering everyday life: from what films to watch, to how to dress, to a ban on taking part in certain events, such as national ceremonies or religious holidays. The group enforces compliance with these rules, and disobedience is treated as sin.

"A Christian woman might ask herself a few questions, such as: 'What does my surrounding think of my clothing? Aren't members of the congregation embarrassed, uneasy, or ashamed because of my appearance?' And even if she has the right to some particular style of dress or hairstyle, she will certainly not insist on it once she notices negative reactions."
"Jehovah's Witnesses are glad to learn the history of their country, but they do not take part in celebrations such as (…) Independence Day."

Isolation from people outside the group: Jehovah's Witnesses are encouraged (and sometimes compelled) to isolate themselves from people outside the organization, including family and friends who are not Jehovah's Witnesses (occasional contact is permitted, but people outside the group cannot be regarded as friends). Those who choose to leave the group are often rejected and treated as "sinners," and all contact with them is severed — even the closest family ties.

"Do our contacts with relatives and neighbors still look the same as before we became Christians? Shouldn't we instead review these relationships and consciously set appropriate boundaries?"
"Don't look for excuses to communicate with a disfellowshipped relative, for example through text messages or emails."
"Frankly speaking, if your friends do not love Jehovah, you do not need them."

2. Information Control

Members are discouraged from reading material critical of the organization. Outside information, including academic or press material, is often labeled "apostasy," and spreading it can result in exclusion. The only accepted source of knowledge is the Watch Tower Society's publications. In the case of Jehovah's Witnesses, we observe:

Lack of access to alternative sources of information: Jehovah's Witnesses are encouraged (and sometimes outright required) to rely exclusively on literature published by the organization (e.g., the Watchtower and Awake! magazines). Literature from outside the organization, including academic books or theological studies (especially ones critical of the Witnesses), is treated as harmful and unworthy of attention.

"Trying to refute the arguments of apostates or of those who are critical of Jehovah's organization will do us no good. Delving into the information they disseminate — whether in printed or electronic form — is wrong and constitutes a spiritual danger."
"We should study the Bible with regard to the topic that concerns us. We can look for additional information using the various tools that God's organization provides."

Demonizing opponents: Everything coming from outside the organization is presented as "false" or "harmful," and people who criticize the organization are called "lawless ones" or "antichrists." The group shows a strong tendency to isolate its members from other worldviews.

"Apostates are 'mentally diseased.'"

Manipulating the organization's history: Jehovah's Witnesses are taught that only their organization holds the true biblical teachings. Its history, as well as any controversies associated with the organization, are meticulously hidden or presented favorably, so as to maintain members' belief in its "infallibility."

3. Thought Control

The community shapes members' thinking through regular meetings, Bible studies, and the repetition of doctrinal statements. Critical thinking about doctrine is condemned, and spiritual doubts are treated as a sign of weak faith or Satan's influence. Examples of thought control:

A black-and-white view of the world: Jehovah's Witnesses are taught that only their organization has the true understanding of the Bible, while the rest of the world is mistaken. Other religious denominations and political views are considered "false" and dangerous. This creates a black-and-white picture of reality, in which everything outside the organization is bad.

"Jehovah (…) has freed us from Satan's world."
"We should try to motivate them [people being recruited] to put into practice what they are learning, to dedicate themselves to Jehovah, and to get baptized. Only then will they survive Jehovah's day."
"A coalition of nations will attack God's people, and Jehovah will execute judgment on the wicked at Armageddon."

The prohibition of doubt: Doubts about the teachings of Jehovah's Witnesses are treated as evidence of a lack of faith and disobedience toward God. The group tries to eliminate all forms of critical thinking, and people who begin to doubt are often seen as a threat and may be disfellowshipped.

"Raising doubts is one of Satan's most effective methods."

Thinking in line with the organization: Jehovah's Witnesses are required to strictly follow the organization's teachings, and any independent thinking or expression of opinions contrary to what the organization teaches is treated as a sign of "rebellion."

"The instructions from Jehovah's organization, on which our lives will depend, may by human judgment seem impractical at the time. We must all be ready to obey any instruction we receive, regardless of whether it seems reasonable from our own or a strategic point of view."

4. Emotional Control

The organization employs strong emotional control, including guilt and fear of being disfellowshipped or of Armageddon. Members often live in a state of emotional tension stemming from fear of condemnation or of losing their social ties after being expelled from the organization. Here are some techniques used to control emotions:

Instilling guilt and shame: Members who don't follow the rules feel guilty and ashamed of their behavior. In addition, a member who leaves the organization may feel "rejected" by God and by loved ones. Disfellowshipped members are treated as "unfaithful," and their emotions are manipulated so that they feel guilty for leaving.

"Hardened sinners (…) pose a moral and spiritual danger to others (…) Like phalanx soldiers who abandon their shields, they endanger not only themselves but also their companions."

Fear of exclusion and ostracism: People who have left the organization are often excluded from social life, which brings enormous emotional stress. Losing contact with family and friends who remain in the organization can lead to a feeling of complete isolation and abandonment.

"Unfortunately, some who have committed serious sins are removed from the congregation. In such a situation, 'we stop associating with them.'"
"A hardened sinner must be disfellowshipped — cut off from contact with Jehovah's clean people."

Maintaining emotional dependence on the group: The group has created a system in which members make their emotions and sense of worth dependent on others' approval. The group provides a sense of community, but only on the condition of full loyalty and obedience.

"There was a time when I did not know what happiness was, even within my own family. I saw love in action for the first time among Jehovah's Witnesses. Anyone who wants to be truly happy and content should find their place in the spiritual paradise."

How to recognize a cult using the BITE model?

Jehovah's Witnesses are an example of a group that uses many techniques of psychological manipulation, leading to full control over its members. Using the BITE model, we can identify the following characteristic elements:

  • Behavior control: mandatory meetings, strict rules governing everyday life, and restricted contact with people outside the group.
  • Information control: isolation from outside sources of information, demonizing opponents, and manipulating the organization's history.
  • Thought control: imposing a black-and-white view of the world, forbidding doubt, and requiring thinking that conforms to the group's doctrine.
  • Emotion control: manipulating members' emotions, instilling guilt, fear, and shame, and fear of being disfellowshipped.

If the group you belong to starts using similar manipulation techniques, this may be a sign that you are dealing with a cult or a destructive group.

Applying the BITE model in psychological practice — the case of Jehovah's Witnesses

Stephen Hassan's BITE model is an effective analytical tool that allows a psychologist to assess to what extent a given social, religious, or ideological group controls an individual through four areas: behavior, information, thought, and emotion.

In psychological practice, the BITE model can be used on many levels — both diagnostic and therapeutic. It helps identify how entangled an individual is in a relationship with a destructive group, and it also supports the process of leaving that relationship and rebuilding psychological autonomy.

1. Diagnosis and recognition of entanglement

A psychologist can use the BITE model to ask precise diagnostic questions. In the case of Jehovah's Witnesses, these might include:

  • Do you feel compelled to attend meetings several times a week?
  • Can you contact people outside the community without discomfort, including family members who aren't Witnesses?
  • How do you react to religious literature from outside your organization, especially material critical of the Witnesses?
  • Have you ever felt guilt or shame after missing a meeting or having doubts about the organization's teachings?

Such questions make it possible to assess whether, and to what extent, a person is under the influence of a control system typical of a destructive group.

2. Psychoeducation and demystifying the group's influence

The BITE model is a useful tool for psychoeducation. In conversation with a patient, a psychologist can show how Jehovah's Witnesses:

  • Control behavior — by requiring participation in preaching, limiting social contacts, or imposing strict rules on dress, sexuality, holiday observance, or education.
  • Control information — by requiring the exclusive use of the organization's literature and warning against "apostate" content online or in academic literature.
  • Control thought — by teaching that only the organization holds "the one truth," and that any doubt is a sin and proof of unfaithfulness toward God.
  • Control emotion — by instilling fear of Armageddon, rejection by family, or exclusion from the community, and by reinforcing guilt over "disobedience."

Demystifying these mechanisms helps a person entangled in the group understand that many of their beliefs, fears, and reactions did not originate within themselves, but were imposed on them as part of the control system.

3. Support in the process of leaving and rebuilding identity

Patients who leave the Jehovah's Witnesses often experience the trauma of leaving, an intense sense of emptiness, loss, and disorientation. The BITE model helps them organize their experiences and make sense of them. A psychologist can help them:

  • recognize and reframe beliefs imposed by the organization;
  • work through fear of divine punishment and social exclusion;
  • reclaim the right to think independently and make their own decisions;
  • strengthen their psychological boundaries and rebuild a sense of agency.

Example: a person who has not celebrated birthdays or holidays for years out of fear of "disobeying God" may, with a therapist's support, consider whether their decision still stems from their own beliefs or from fear instilled by the organization.

4. Support for families and loved ones

The BITE model can also be used when working with the families of people recruited by Jehovah's Witnesses. It helps loved ones understand that such a person's behavior doesn't necessarily stem from their character, but from the manipulative system in which they function. A psychologist can help the family:

  • develop a strategy for staying in contact without violating emotional boundaries;
  • avoid confrontation that could push the person deeper into isolation within the group;
  • recognize signs of openness to change and support the process of leaving the group.

Summary

The BITE model is an extremely helpful tool for psychologists working with people entangled in destructive groups such as Jehovah's Witnesses. It enables not only the diagnosis and analysis of the manipulation mechanisms used by the group, but also real help in regaining psychological independence, rebuilding identity, and healing spiritual and emotional trauma.

A psychologist who uses the BITE model can serve as a guide and a source of support in the process of leaving psychological bondage — leading the patient from dependence toward freedom, from fear toward trust in themselves.

Discussion

The BITE model proves to be an effective tool for analyzing the mechanisms of psychological influence within religious groups. In the case of Jehovah's Witnesses, numerous practices can be identified that correspond to the individual categories of control described by Hassan. While belonging to the community does not in itself necessarily mean experiencing psychological abuse, many accounts from former members point to a deep, destructive impact of the organization on the individual.

Conclusions

Applying the BITE model makes it possible to understand how certain religious groups can exert excessive influence over their members. The case study of Jehovah's Witnesses shows that even a legally operating organization can display the characteristics of a destructive group. Regardless of any spiritual assessment, it is worthwhile, from a psychological point of view, to study the mechanisms of influence used within religious communities.
